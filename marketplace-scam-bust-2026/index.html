1. NEWDERY ZHX-PB22 power bank — the recall that took a federal warning to force

The listingA black power bank with bright yellow cables, sold exclusively on Amazon for $27–$50.

The pitchCheap portable power from a brand you'd never heard of — 21,000+ units sold, so it looked established.

Who it's aimed atAnyone who's ever thought "it's just a battery, how different can they be?"

Verified facts

CPSC recall 26-792, issued September 24, 2026. About 21,380 units. Nine reports of the banks exploding and igniting — including one burn injury and one serious fire that caused an estimated $2 million in property damage. Sold on Amazon from October 2023 to November 2024. Identify it by the model number ZHX-PB22 printed on the back, "NEWDERY" engraved on the front, black body with yellow cables. The part that should worry you: the CPSC first issued a Product Safety Warning back in June 2025 because the importer refused to conduct a recall. The actual recall came fifteen months later.

Why it looked tempting

$27–$50 is real money saved versus a name-brand bank; 21,000+ units sold made it look like a proven product, not a gamble; it was on Amazon, which plenty of shoppers treat as a safety signal.

Why you shouldn't buy it

Nine fires, a $2 million house fire, a burn injury — documented, not alleged; the company fought the recall for over a year, which tells you everything about who's behind the listing.

Verdict

If you own one, stop using it immediately and claim the full refund — NEWDERY requires a photo of the unit showing the model number, serial number, and the word "recalled" plus your last name written on it in permanent marker (newdery.com recall page; 888-256-6673). Never buy a battery from a brand that needed a federal warning to do the right thing.

2. Hererod PW40 / EW40 jump starter — the warning with no refund

The listingA portable car jump starter that doubles as a power bank, sold with jumper cables on Amazon, CarKart, and eBay.

The pitchStart your car and charge your phone from one brick — emergency gear without the NOCO price tag.

Who it's aimed atDrivers who want a jump starter in the trunk "just in case" and shop on price.

Verified facts

CPSC Product Safety Warning 26-768, issued September 17, 2026 — a warning, NOT a recall. About 12,500 units. The CPSC is aware of 16 fire reports, including seven with property damage. Sold online from August 2024 through August 2026. The PW40 has "PW40" printed on the front and a tire-inflation function; the EW40 carries no model marking and no inflation. The manufacturer never responded to the CPSC's recall or information requests, so there is no repair, no replacement, and no refund program. Nothing.

Why it looked tempting

Jump starter + power bank + tire inflator in one box; far cheaper than established emergency-gear brands; thousands of units sold across three marketplaces looked like traction.

Why you shouldn't buy it

Sixteen fires, and the company behind it simply vanished when the federal safety agency came calling; a warning with no remedy is the worst outcome on this entire list — you can't even get your money back.

Verdict

If you own one, stop using it entirely — no charging, no jump-starting, no tire inflation — and take it to a household hazardous-waste center (never the trash or a retail battery bin). When you buy emergency gear, the brand still existing after the sale IS the product.

3. XO Poppy Power Trip magnetic wireless power bank — the $15 checkout trap

The listingA cute little magnetic wireless power bank — cream, pink-bow print, or teddy-bear print — model PYPBK5M.

The pitchFifteen bucks at the TJX or Marshalls checkout. An impulse buy, barely a decision.

Who it's aimed atAnyone killing time in a checkout line. The definition of an unconsidered purchase.

Verified facts

CPSC recall 26-740, issued September 3, 2026. About 32,400 units. Sold at TJX and Marshalls stores nationwide from April 2025 through March 2026 for about $15 each. The battery can overheat and ignite — no incidents were reported before the recall, so this one was caught early. Full refund as a virtual gift card usable anywhere Mastercard is accepted. Identify it by model PYPBK5M printed on the bottom of the original packaging, directly above the barcode.

Why it looked tempting

$15 — below the threshold where anyone researches anything; adorable designs that read as harmless; it was on a real store shelf, which feels like a safety signal.

Why you shouldn't buy it

A real store shelf is not a safety certification — TJX is a retailer, not a test lab; 32,400 units of a fire-hazard battery moved through checkout lines in under a year.

Verdict

Caught early is still recalled. Check your junk drawer — this is exactly the kind of thing people forget they own, and the one you'd never think to Google for a recall.

4. Esgofo "100,000-lumen" flashlight ($16) — the listing that disproves itself

The listingThe Esgofo rechargeable LED flashlight on Amazon — $16 on a 44%-off deal.

The pitch"Up to 100,000 lumens" with "3,280 feet" of throw. A pocket sun for sixteen dollars.

Who it's aimed atAnyone who sorts flashlights by "brightest" and trusts the biggest number.

Verified facts

The listing's own specs say 50W LED and a 6,000mAh battery. White LEDs deliver roughly 100–150 lumens per watt, so a 50W LED caps honest output at about 5,000–7,500 lumens — more than ten times short of the headline claim. A genuine 100,000 lumens needs roughly 700–1,000 watts of LED power; a 6,000mAh cell holds about 22 watt-hours, which couldn't feed that for more than a couple of minutes — and 700W of heat in a six-inch aluminum tube is pure fantasy. Real 100,000-lumen lights like the Imalent MS18 use 18 LEDs, eight 21700 cells, built-in cooling fans, and weigh around 1.8kg. Autoblog's October 7 deals piece repeated the 100,000-lumen claim completely uncritically.

Why it looked tempting

Sixteen dollars for a number that sounds like a searchlight; the "44% off" framing manufactures urgency; it looks like a normal tactical flashlight, not an obvious joke.

Why you shouldn't buy it

The listing contradicts itself — its own 50W spec disproves its own 100,000-lumen headline; when a listing's specs disagree with each other, believe the smaller number, then keep scrolling.

Verdict

Buy lumens from brands that publish ANSI FL1 test data, not from whoever shouts the biggest number. The honest 5,000-lumen light at this price would still be a fine deal; the lie is the product.

5. "Million-lumen" budget flashlights — measured at 0.05% of the claim

The listingNo-name Amazon flashlights advertising one to two-and-a-half million lumens for under $50. One brand, Gekiro, sold a $29.99 light claiming 2.5 million lumens.

The pitchSearchlight power for thirty bucks. The numbers do all the selling.

Who it's aimed atGift buyers and spec-sheet shoppers who'll never own an integrating sphere.

Verified facts

1Lumen's testing puts the brightest flashlights on Earth at roughly 200,000–230,000 lumens — bulky, expensive, fan-cooled instruments, not $30 Amazon finds. The Torque Test Channel measured several budget "million-lumen" Amazon lights properly: the best managed 0.24% of its advertised output, the worst 0.05%. Some sellers raised the advertised numbers on unchanged products between the order date and the test date.

Why it looked tempting

The numbers are thrilling and the price is trivial; they look like serious tactical hardware in the photos; thousands of listings repeat the same claims, which creates false consensus.

Why you shouldn't buy it

Under a quarter of one percent of the advertised output, measured — that isn't exaggeration, it's fiction with a shipping box; sellers editing specs upward on identical hardware proves no one behind these listings is measuring anything.

Verdict

If the lumen number has more digits than the price has dollars, walk away. Real brightness has real heatsinks, real batteries, and real test data.

6. Fake-capacity power banks ("20,000mAh" for $10) — the teardown doesn't lie

The listingNo-name "20,000mAh" power banks on eBay and marketplace stalls for around $10–$20.

The pitchTen full phone charges for the price of lunch.

Who it's aimed atTravelers and students buying purely on price.

Verified facts

PhoneArena bought one for about $10, tested it, and tore it down — it managed barely two iPhone charges before dying, roughly one-fifth of the claimed capacity. Inside: four unlabeled 18650 cells of unknown quality, which couldn't deliver the advertised capacity even if they were premium cells. A true 100,000mAh bank would need a backpack to carry; a "50,000mAh" bank the size of a deck of cards would hold about 185Wh — physically implausible and illegal to take on a plane.

Why it looked tempting

The price-to-capacity ratio looks like 5× the value of a real bank; "20,000mAh" is a concrete number that feels technical and trustworthy; for a backup battery, "good enough" feels like a reasonable gamble.

Why you shouldn't buy it

Mystery cells are exactly the cells that vent and burn — the same failure mode as the recalled banks above, just without a brand name to attach a recall to; capacity claims are the easiest lie in the business because almost nobody tests them.

Verdict

Buy power banks from brands with a reputation to lose. The premium isn't for the logo — it's for cells that were actually manufactured this decade by someone accountable.

The 5 red flags: a field guide to the next scam listing

Do the watt math. Lumens divided by 100 is the minimum LED wattage an honest claim needs. If the listing's own wattage spec is ten times short of its lumen headline — Esgofo's 50W versus 100,000 lumens — the lumens are fiction. The listing just told on itself.

Do the capacity math. A "50,000mAh" bank the size of a deck of cards would hold ~185Wh: too heavy to hold comfortably and illegal to fly with. Real capacity has real size and real weight. If the numbers and the photos disagree, trust physics.

Check the certification marks — then check the marks. In January 2026, Amazon and UL Solutions jointly sued sellers putting counterfeit UL certification marks on e-bikes and e-scooters sold on Amazon; a permanent injunction followed in July 2026 across seven listings. A UL logo baked into a listing image is a claim, not a certificate — look it up in UL's own database.

Google the brand plus "recall" before you buy. NEWDERY fought its recall for fifteen months after a federal warning. Hererod ghosted the CPSC entirely. A brand with no physical presence and no customer service cannot be held accountable, and batteries are the wrong place to learn that.

Beware the number that moved. Torque Test Channel caught sellers raising lumen claims on unchanged products between order and test. If a listing's specs inflate over time with no new model behind them, you're watching marketing, not engineering.

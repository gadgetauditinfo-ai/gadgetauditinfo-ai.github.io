How to choose a power bank you can actually fly with

Watt-hours, output and weight matter more than the capacity number on the box.

A power bank can advertise a huge milliamp-hour number and still be the wrong travel choice. Airlines reason in watt-hours, laptops care about USB-C output, and your shoulder notices weight long before a product page does.

This guide separates those decisions. It is a product review based on official specifications and campaign material; no bench sample has been received, so the measured-data column stays blank rather than borrowing manufacturer claims.

Start with watt-hours, not milliamp-hours

Milliamp-hours are only meaningful beside battery voltage. Watt-hours describe stored energy in the unit airlines use. A pack that clearly prints its Wh rating saves time at security and makes the comparison honest.

Match output to the device

A phone can live with modest USB-C output. A laptop may negotiate 65W, 100W or 140W. Buying extra capacity without enough output gives you a large battery that still cannot sustain the machine you carry.

Treat weight as a daily specification

A 25,000mAh pack is bag gear, not pocket gear. If the battery only rescues a phone, a smaller magnetic pack is often the better tool because it is the one you will actually carry.

Read the label before you reach security

The watt-hour rating that matters is the one printed on the battery itself — that is what the security agent reads. Find it before you pack: it is usually molded into the casing or printed on a label near the capacity figure. If the print has worn off, photograph the product page showing the Wh rating and keep the manual in your bag. An unlabeled high-capacity pack invites the exact conversation you want to avoid.

Know the two thresholds. Up to 100Wh, spare batteries fly in carry-on baggage as a matter of course. Between 100Wh and 160Wh, most airlines allow up to two spares with prior approval — which means asking before the travel day, not at the gate. Above 160Wh, the pack stays home on passenger flights. These are IATA-based norms, but the airline’s published policy is the final word.

Spare lithium batteries always ride in the cabin, never in checked baggage — this is the rule with the least flexibility in all of air travel. Pack the bank where you can reach it: top of the bag, outer pocket, easy to present. And remember the limit applies per battery, not per passenger total, so two 90Wh packs are generally fine where one 185Wh pack is not.

Charging on board is a separate question from carrying. Seat power varies wildly by airline and aircraft, and a power bank that is legal to carry may still be restricted from use during certain flight phases. When in doubt, carry the bank, charge from the seat where offered, and save the bank’s capacity for the layover.

Flaws, but not deal-breakers

The UGREEN is still a heavy object, and the display adds complexity. Airline and carrier rules can change, so a compliant capacity does not replace checking the carrier before departure.

Frequently asked questions

Is 25,000mAh allowed on a plane?

Capacity alone is not enough. Check the printed watt-hour rating and your airline’s current policy. The UGREEN model covered here is listed as 90Wh in our source material.

Can I put a power bank in checked baggage?

Portable lithium batteries are generally expected in carry-on baggage, not checked baggage. Confirm the exact policy with your airline before travel.

Why is the measured column empty?

No physical sample has reached our bench. We label official specifications as claims and refuse to present them as measurements.

What if my power bank has no Wh printed on it?

Then you are carrying an argument waiting to happen. Calculate the watt-hours from the milliamp-hour rating and nominal voltage if you can, screenshot the manufacturer’s specification page, and consider replacing the pack — reputable brands print the Wh rating on the unit precisely because airlines ask for it.

Can I bring two power banks on one flight?

Generally yes, if each is within the limit — the 100Wh threshold applies per battery. Two 90Wh packs in carry-on baggage are normally fine; confirm with your airline’s published battery policy before travel, since individual carriers can be stricter than the IATA baseline.

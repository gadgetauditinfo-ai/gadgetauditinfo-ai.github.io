Your sleep data is the most personal data you own. It knows when you’re unconscious, where you live, and whether your heart is misbehaving. So this guide starts with the question most sleep-tech roundups bury: what does it cost you, and what does it cost every month?

These are the seven sleep gadgets actually worth buying in twenty twenty-six. Every one is buyable right now at a verified price, and every subscription situation is stated up front. If a device needs a subscription to do the thing it was sold for, you’ll hear the three-year cost. And nothing here is crowdfunded, on pre-order, or “launching soon.” A sleep gadget that doesn’t exist yet can’t fix your Tuesday night.

First: the Hatch Sleep Clock, the one that watches your sleep without touching you.

This is a bedside sleep tracker that monitors breathing and movement with radar. There’s no camera, no microphone pointed at you, and nothing to wear. It tracks sleep stages, breathing, and movement from your nightstand, then builds a nightly plan and a sunrise alarm around what it measured. Hatch cites ninety-seven percent sleep-detection accuracy versus clinical polysomnography, based on its Somnofy validation.

This is for the person who wants sleep data but refuses to wear a ring or watch in bed, and doesn’t want a camera or listening microphone on the nightstand. It costs two hundred forty-nine dollars and ninety-nine cents. Sleep tracking, insights, and the nightly plan need no subscription. Hatch Plus is optional at five dollars and ninety-nine cents a month, or fifty-nine dollars and ninety-nine cents a year, and buys access to the sound library. Hatch’s own pages have shown conflicting Hatch Plus prices, so verify that part at checkout.

The verdict: this is the best buyable contactless sleep tracker, and the only pick that never touches you. Buy it if wearing a tracker to bed is a non-starter. Skip it if your smartwatch already does this job.

Second: the Soundcore Sleep A thirty, the active-noise-cancelling sleep earbud that became the reference.

It combines smart noise cancellation up to thirty decibels with adaptive snore masking. The case listens to the room and the buds mask what it hears. It also tracks sleep and position in the Soundcore app, in a three-gram-per-bud package that’s about seven percent slimmer than the previous generation. Battery life is roughly nine hours in the buds and forty-five hours total with the case. Bluetooth is version five point four.

This is for light sleepers whose enemy is noise: snoring partners, thin walls, or city hum. The list price is one hundred ninety-nine dollars and ninety-nine cents at Soundcore, with no subscription. It dropped to one hundred fifty-nine dollars and ninety-nine cents during Prime Big Deal Days, but we couldn’t verify that discount today, so the list price is the honest number.

One warning: Soundcore has already announced the Sleep Earbuds four and four Pro at two hundred twenty-nine dollars and ninety-nine cents and three hundred forty-nine dollars and ninety-nine cents. Those are expected in October and November twenty twenty-six. If you want heart-rate data, waiting for the four Pro could make sense. If noise is ruining your sleep now, the A thirty is the safest mainstream recommendation.

Third: Ozlo Sleepbuds two, the premium passive pick from the heirs to the old Bose Sleepbuds idea.

Ozlo skips active noise cancellation and uses passive isolation plus adaptive sound masking. It also measures your room’s temperature, light, noise, and humidity, so the app can help explain why a night went badly, not just report that it did. The buds stream for fourteen hours on one charge, the case holds two to three nights, there are four tip sizes, and a case button lets you snooze or start masking sound without reaching for your phone.

Ozlo costs two hundred seventy-nine dollars with no subscription. It’s the premium pick for masking-sound sleepers and side sleepers who dislike the pressure of active noise cancellation. But it costs eighty dollars more than the Soundcore, and it doesn’t track heart rate. If active-noise-cancelling buds feel uncomfortable after hours in bed, Ozlo is your lane. Otherwise, the A thirty is the better value.

Fourth: the QALO S L one Smart Band, the subscription-free tracker that undercuts Oura.

QALO made its name in rings, then entered screenless health tracking with a simple weapon: no subscription. The band tracks sleep stages and scores, heart rate, heart-rate variability, blood oxygen, stress, skin temperature, and women’s health. It rolls sleep, movement, stress, and recovery into a proprietary Q Score from one to one hundred, and it runs for more than twelve days per charge.

This is for someone who wants Oura’s basic job done without Oura’s rent. The S L one is screenless, uses a magnetic silicone strap, is water-resistant, and can share one app and health profile with QALO’s smart ring. It costs one hundred ninety-nine dollars and ninety-five cents, with no subscription, and it is F S A and H S A eligible.

The trade-off is trust. The Q Score is a black box, QALO’s sleep algorithms don’t have Oura’s track record, and there’s no cited clinical validation yet. But if subscriptions offend you and you don’t need a ring, this is the tracked-sleep pick.

Fifth: the Somnee Smart Sleep Headband, the neurostimulation outlier with a membership tax.

Somnee is the only product in this guide that tries to change your sleep instead of simply measuring it. The company was founded by Berkeley sleep scientist Doctor Matthew Walker. Its headband runs a fifteen-minute pre-bed session of personalized transcranial alternating-current stimulation, with clinical-grade E E G and bone-conduction speakers. You don’t wear it overnight. It’s a pre-sleep intervention, not a diary.

This is for the buyer who has tried trackers and earbuds and still struggles to fall asleep. Somnee claims about fifty percent faster sleep onset and about thirty-three percent fewer wake-ups. Those are company claims, and the clinical literature on consumer neurostimulation for insomnia is still thin.

Pricing starts at three hundred eighty-nine dollars plus membership. The Smart Sleep Bundle costs four hundred eighty-nine dollars and includes six months of membership. Membership is required for daily personalization, but the ongoing rate is unverified. That missing number is a serious problem. The device is H S A and F S A eligible, but it’s still the most expensive entry in this guide before the recurring cost.

The verdict: it’s the most interesting device here and the hardest to recommend. Consider it only if sleep onset is your actual problem and you’ve exhausted the cheaper fixes. Don’t buy until Somnee publishes the ongoing membership price plainly.

Sixth: the Oura Ring five, the contrast anchor — the best tracker that charges rent.

Oura made sleep tracking mainstream. This generation is forty percent thinner than Ring four, at two point two eight millimeters, with a six-to-nine-day battery, titanium construction, and one-hundred-meter water resistance. It tracks sleep stages, readiness, heart rate, heart-rate variability, nighttime blood-pressure signals, and breathing through Health Radar. Those blood-pressure signals have a regional limit: United States, India, and the United Arab Emirates.

The base Black and Silver finishes cost three hundred ninety-nine dollars. Gold, Stealth, and Deep Rose cost four hundred ninety-nine dollars. Full features require a subscription at five dollars and ninety-nine cents per month, or sixty-nine dollars and ninety-nine cents per year. Three years with the base ring costs about six hundred nine dollars. The portable charging case is another ninety-nine dollars.

Oura remains the most proven consumer sleep algorithm in the category. If you want that reference-grade experience and the subscription doesn’t bother you, it’s still the ring to beat. If the subscription does bother you, QALO costs one hundred ninety-nine dollars and ninety-five cents with no rent.

Seventh: SleepPhones Wireless, the ninety-nine-dollar-and-ninety-five-cent budget pick that isn’t a compromise.

SleepPhones is a washable fleece or breeze headband with removable Bluetooth speakers. It plays your own sleep sounds, white noise, or podcasts through flat speakers and runs for up to ten hours per charge. A full charge takes about three hours. The sleep-sound downloads and app are free.

This is for budget buyers and side sleepers. Flat speakers in fabric can be more comfortable on a pillow than anything inside the ear. The price is ninety-nine dollars and ninety-five cents, with no subscription.

The limitation is simple: it’s not a tracker. It collects no sleep data, has no smart masking, and offers no active noise cancellation. It just plays sound in the most side-sleeper-friendly form factor under one hundred dollars. For about a third of Ozlo’s price, that’s a fair trade.

There is one medical-channel alternative worth knowing: Happy Ring.

Happy Ring costs three hundred ninety-six dollars through Happy Health and is F D A cleared, Class two, for at-home sleep-apnea diagnosis. Its artificial intelligence analyzes your data, and a physician in Happy Health’s network can diagnose and prescribe a C P A P machine or dental device. The company claims about ninety-seven percent accuracy versus lab studies. The price includes one year of sleep tracking and physician consultations, and it is often covered by insurance. Pricing after the first year is unverified, so ask before buying. This isn’t a normal retail gadget, which is why it sits outside the seven ranked picks. But if your concern is sleep apnea rather than poor habits, a diagnosis matters more than any consumer dashboard.

The short version: Hatch is the contactless buy. Soundcore is the noise-first recommendation. Ozlo is the premium passive option for side sleepers. QALO is the no-subscription tracker. Somnee is the intervention for sleep-onset problems, but only after it states the membership price. Oura remains the proven algorithm with rent attached. And SleepPhones is the honest under-one-hundred-dollar audio pick.

Whatever you choose, remember the difference between tracking and diagnosis. Except for Happy Ring’s medical channel, these devices estimate and coach. They do not replace a doctor. If you stop breathing at night, see a clinician, not a roundup.
